### Q1. Which of the following liquid pairs shows a negative deviation from Raoult's law?
* **Options:**
  (a) Ethanol + Acetone  
  (b) Benzene + Toluene  
  (c) Acetone + Chloroform  
  (d) Nitromethane + Acetone  
* **Correct Option:** **(c) Acetone + Chloroform**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  In a mixture of acetone and chloroform, the intermolecular attractive forces between acetone (A) and chloroform (B) are stronger than the A-A (acetone-acetone) and B-B (chloroform-chloroform) attractions in the pure liquids. This occurs because the hydrogen atom of chloroform forms a strong hydrogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of acetone:
  $$\text{Cl}_3\text{C-H}\cdots\text{O}=\text{C}(\text{CH}_3)_2$$
  As a result, the escaping tendency of both molecules decreases, lowering the overall vapor pressure of the solution compared to that predicted by Raoult's law. This represents a **negative deviation**.

### Q2. The value of Henry's Law constant ($K_H$):
* **Options:**
  (a) increases with increase in temperature.  
  (b) decreases with increase in temperature.  
  (c) remains constant with temperature.  
  (d) first increases then decreases.  
* **Correct Option:** **(a) increases with increase in temperature.**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  According to Henry's Law:
  $$p = K_H \cdot x$$
  where $p$ is the partial pressure of the gas and $x$ is its mole fraction (solubility) in the liquid. 
  The solubility of a gas in a liquid is an exothermic process ($\Delta H_{\text{sol}} < 0$). According to Le Chatelier's Principle, as temperature increases, the solubility ($x$) of the gas decreases for a given partial pressure ($p$). Since $x = p / K_H$, a decrease in $x$ at a constant $p$ mathematically requires an **increase in the Henry's law constant ($K_H$)**. Thus, $K_H$ increases with an increase in temperature.

### Q3. An isotonic solution has the same:
* **Options:**
  (a) Vapor pressure  
  (b) Osmotic pressure  
  (c) Boiling point elevation  
  (d) Freezing point depression  
* **Correct Option:** **(b) Osmotic pressure**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  Isotonic solutions are defined as solutions that possess the same osmotic pressure ($\Pi$) at a given temperature. If two solutions are separated by a semipermeable membrane, no net osmosis (flow of solvent) occurs between them because their osmotic pressures are equal ($\Pi_1 = \Pi_2$). For non-electrolytes, this also implies they have the same molar concentration ($C_1 = C_2$).

### Q4. The SI unit of molar conductivity ($\Lambda_m$) is:
* **Options:**
  (a) $\text{S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$  
  (b) $\text{S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$  
  (c) $\text{S}^{-1}\text{ m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$  
  (d) $\Omega^{-1}\text{ cm}^{-1}$  
* **Correct Option:** **(b) $\text{S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  Molar conductivity is given by the relation:
  $$\Lambda_m = \frac{\kappa}{C}$$
  In SI units:
  * Conductivity ($\kappa$) is expressed in Siemens per meter ($\text{S m}^{-1}$).
  * Concentration ($C$) is expressed in moles per cubic meter ($\text{mol m}^{-3}$).
  
  Substituting these into the equation:
  $$\Lambda_m = \frac{\text{S m}^{-1}}{\text{mol m}^{-3}} = \text{S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$$
  While $\text{S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$ is a very common practical unit, **$\text{S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$** is the official **SI unit**.

### Q5. How many Coulombs of electricity are required for the reduction of 1 mole of $\text{MnO}_4^-$ to $\text{Mn}^{2+}$?
* **Options:**
  (a) $96,500\text{ C}$  
  (b) $1,93,000\text{ C}$  
  (c) $4,82,500\text{ C}$  
  (d) $5,79,000\text{ C}$  
* **Correct Option:** **(c) $4,82,500\text{ C}$**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  Write the balanced reduction half-reaction:
  $$\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  In this reaction, manganese is reduced from an oxidation state of $+7$ (in $\text{MnO}_4^-$) to $+2$ (in $\text{Mn}^{2+}$). This process requires $5$ moles of electrons per mole of $\text{MnO}_4^-$:
  $$\text{Quantity of electricity } (Q) = n \cdot F$$
  $$Q = 5 \text{ mol } e^- \times 96,500\text{ C mol}^{-1} = 4,82,500\text{ C}$$

### Q6. The cell constant of a conductivity cell:
* **Options:**
  (a) changes with change of electrolyte.  
  (b) changes with change of concentration.  
  (c) changes with temperature.  
  (d) remains constant for a given cell.  
* **Correct Option:** **(d) remains constant for a given cell.**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  The cell constant ($G^*$) of a conductivity cell is given by the geometric ratio:
  $$G^* = \frac{l}{A}$$
  where $l$ is the distance between the two electrodes and $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the electrodes. Since $l$ and $A$ are fixed physical dimensions of a given cell, the value of the cell constant **remains constant** regardless of the nature, concentration, or temperature of the electrolyte placed inside it.

### Q7. The half-life period ($t_{1/2}$) of a first-order reaction is independent of:
* **Options:**
  (a) Rate constant  
  (b) Initial concentration of reactant  
  (c) Temperature  
  (d) Activation energy  
* **Correct Option:** **(b) Initial concentration of reactant**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  For a first-order reaction, the half-life period ($t_{1/2}$) is given by:
  $$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}$$
  where $k$ is the rate constant. 
  This mathematical expression shows that $t_{1/2}$ depends solely on $k$. It does not contain any term representing the initial concentration of the reactant ($[A]_0$). Hence, it is **independent of the initial concentration**. (Note: $k$ itself depends on temperature and activation energy, making $t_{1/2}$ dependent on them as well).

### Q8. If the concentration of a reactant is increased by a factor of 4 and the rate of reaction increases by a factor of 2, the order of reaction is:
* **Options:**
  (a) 2  
  (b) 1  
  (c) 0.5  
  (d) 0  
* **Correct Option:** **(c) 0.5**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  Let the rate law of the reaction be:
  $$\text{Rate } (R) = k[A]^n$$
  where $n$ is the order of the reaction.
  When the concentration of reactant $A$ is increased by a factor of 4, the new rate ($R'$) is:
  $$R' = k(4[A])^n = 4^n \cdot (k[A]^n) = 4^n \cdot R$$
  We are given that the rate increases by a factor of 2 ($R' = 2R$). Therefore:
  $$4^n = 2 \Rightarrow (2^2)^n = 2^1 \Rightarrow 2n = 1 \Rightarrow n = 0.5$$
  The order of the reaction is **0.5** (or $1/2$).

### Q9. According to Arrhenius equation, the slope of the plot of $\ln k$ vs $1/T$ is equal to:
* **Options:**
  (a) $-E_a / R$  
  (b) $E_a / R$  
  (c) $-E_a / 2.303R$  
  (d) $A / R$  
* **Correct Option:** **(a) $-E_a / R$**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  The Arrhenius equation is:
  $$k = A \cdot e^{-E_a / RT}$$
  Taking the natural logarithm ($\ln$) on both sides gives:
  $$\ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R} \left(\frac{1}{T}\right)$$
  Comparing this to the equation of a straight line, $y = mx + c$:
  * $y = \ln k$
  * $x = \frac{1}{T}$
  * $c = \ln A$ (y-intercept)
  * $m = -\frac{E_a}{R}$ (slope)
  
  Thus, the slope is **$-E_a / R$**. (If the graph were plotted as $\log_{10} k$ vs $1/T$, the slope would be $-E_a / 2.303R$).

### Q10. Which of the following alkyl halides undergoes $\text{S}_N1$ reaction fastest?
* **Options:**
  (a) $\text{CH}_3\text{-CH}_2\text{-Cl}$  
  (b) $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH-Cl}$  
  (c) $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br}$  
  (d) $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Cl}$  
* **Correct Option:** **(c) $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br}$**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  $\text{S}_N1$ reactions proceed via a carbocation intermediate, and the rate of reaction depends on the stability of this carbocation:
  1. **Carbocation Stability:** Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alkyl halides form tertiary carbocations, which are highly stabilized by hyperconjugation and inductive effects ($+I$ of three alkyl groups). Thus, $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br}$ and $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Cl}$ are much more reactive than secondary $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH-Cl}$ ($2^\circ$) and primary $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{-Cl}$ ($1^\circ$).
  2. **Leaving Group Ability:** Between $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br}$ and $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Cl}$, the leaving group is bromide ($\text{Br}^-$) and chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$) respectively. Since bromine is larger than chlorine, the $\text{C-Br}$ bond is longer, weaker, and more easily cleaved than the $\text{C-Cl}$ bond. Furthermore, $\text{Br}^-$ is a weaker base and a better leaving group than $\text{Cl}^-$.
  
  Therefore, **$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br}$ (tert-butyl bromide)** undergoes $\text{S}_N1$ substitution fastest.

### Q11. Finkelstein reaction is used for the preparation of:
* **Options:**
  (a) Alkyl fluorides  
  (b) Alkyl iodides  
  (c) Alkyl chlorides  
  (d) Aryl iodides  
* **Correct Option:** **(b) Alkyl iodides**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  The Finkelstein reaction is a halogen exchange reaction specifically used to synthesize alkyl iodides. It involves reacting alkyl chlorides or alkyl bromides with sodium iodide ($\text{NaI}$) in dry acetone:
  $$\text{R-Cl} + \text{NaI} \xrightarrow{\text{acetone}} \text{R-I} + \text{NaCl} \downarrow$$
  $$\text{R-Br} + \text{NaI} \xrightarrow{\text{acetone}} \text{R-I} + \text{NaBr} \downarrow$$
  The precipitation of $\text{NaCl}$ or $\text{NaBr}$ in dry acetone (due to their lower solubility compared to $\text{NaI}$) drives the reaction forward in accordance with Le Chatelier's Principle.

* **Options:**
  (a) Wurtz reaction  
  (b) Fittig reaction  
  (c) Wurtz-Fittig reaction  
  (d) Kolbe's reaction  
* **Correct Option:** **(b) Fittig reaction**
* **Detailed Explanation:** 
  * **Fittig reaction:** The coupling of two aryl halide molecules (e.g., chlorobenzene) with sodium metal in the presence of dry ether to form a diaryl compound (e.g., diphenyl) is called the Fittig reaction:

  * **Wurtz-Fittig reaction** involves a mixture of an alkyl halide and an aryl halide to give an alkylarene.

### Q17. State Raoult's Law for a solution of non-volatile solute. How is the molecular mass of a solute determined using relative lowering of vapor pressure?
* **Solution:**
  1. **Raoult's Law (for non-volatile solutes):**
     At a given temperature, the vapor pressure of a solution containing a non-volatile solute is directly proportional to the mole fraction of the solvent in that solution. 
     Alternatively, the **relative lowering of vapor pressure** of a dilute solution is equal to the mole fraction of the non-volatile solute dissolved in it.
     $$\frac{p_1^\circ - p_1}{p_1^\circ} = x_2$$
     where:
     * $p_1^\circ$ = Vapor pressure of pure solvent.
     * $p_1$ = Vapor pressure of solvent in the solution.
     * $x_2$ = Mole fraction of the non-volatile solute.

  2. **Determination of Molecular Mass ($M_2$):**
     Let $w_2$ and $M_2$ be the mass and molar mass of the solute, respectively.
     Let $w_1$ and $M_1$ be the mass and molar mass of the solvent, respectively.
     The mole fraction of the solute ($x_2$) is:
     $$x_2 = \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$
     where $n_2 = \frac{w_2}{M_2}$ (moles of solute) and $n_1 = \frac{w_1}{M_1}$ (moles of solvent).
     For a very dilute solution, $n_2 \ll n_1$, so we can approximate the denominator:
     $$n_1 + n_2 \approx n_1 \Rightarrow x_2 \approx \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{w_2 / M_2}{w_1 / M_1} = \frac{w_2 \cdot M_1}{w_1 \cdot M_2}$$
     Substituting this into the Raoult's Law expression:
     $$\frac{p_1^\circ - p_1}{p_1^\circ} = \frac{w_2 \cdot M_1}{w_1 \cdot M_2}$$
     Rearranging to solve for the molar mass of the solute ($M_2$):
     $$M_2 = \frac{w_2 \cdot M_1 \cdot p_1^\circ}{w_1 \cdot (p_1^\circ - p_1)}$$

### Q18. Calculate the degree of dissociation ($\alpha$) of $0.00241\text{ M}$ acetic acid if its molar conductivity ($\Lambda_m$) is $39.05\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$. Given: $\Lambda^\circ(\text{H}^+) = 349.6\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$ and $\Lambda^\circ(\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-) = 40.9\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$.
* **Solution:**
  1. **Calculate the limiting molar conductivity ($\Lambda^\circ_m$) of acetic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$):**
     Using Kohlrausch's Law of independent migration of ions:
     $$\Lambda^\circ_m(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = \Lambda^\circ(\text{H}^+) + \Lambda^\circ(\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-)$$
     $$\Lambda^\circ_m(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = 349.6\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1} + 40.9\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1} = 390.5\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$$

  2. **Calculate the degree of dissociation ($\alpha$):**
     $$\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda^\circ_m}$$
     Given that $\Lambda_m = 39.05\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$:
     $$\alpha = \frac{39.05}{390.5} = 0.1$$
  
  * **Answer:** The degree of dissociation of acetic acid is **0.1** (or **10%**).

### Q19. A first-order reaction has a rate constant of $1.15 \times 10^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$. How long will $5.0\text{ g}$ of this reactant take to reduce to $3.0\text{ g}$?
* **Solution:**
  For a first-order reaction, the integrated rate equation is:
  $$t = \frac{2.303}{k} \log_{10} \left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}\right)$$
  Given:
  * Rate constant ($k$) = $1.15 \times 10^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$
  * Initial mass ($[A]_0$) = $5.0\text{ g}$
  * Final mass ($[A]$) = $3.0\text{ g}$

  Substitute the given values into the equation:
  $$t = \frac{2.303}{1.15 \times 10^{-3}} \log_{10} \left(\frac{5.0}{3.0}\right)$$
  $$t = \left(\frac{2.303}{1.15 \times 10^{-3}}\right) \log_{10} (1.6667)$$
  Using logarithmic value:
  $$\log_{10} (1.6667) \approx 0.2218$$
  Now, calculate $t$:
  $$t = \frac{2.303 \times 0.2218}{1.15 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{0.5109}{1.15 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 444.26\text{ seconds}$$
  
  * **Answer:** It will take **$444.3\text{ seconds}$** (or $7.4\text{ minutes}$) for the reactant to reduce from $5.0\text{ g}$ to $3.0\text{ g}$.

### Q20. Give chemical tests to distinguish between:
**(a) Chlorobenzene and Benzyl chloride**  
**(b) Chloroform and Carbon tetrachloride**
* **Solution:**
  **(a) Distinguishing Chlorobenzene and Benzyl chloride:**
  * **Test:** Heat each compound with aqueous sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) solution for a few minutes. Acidify the mixture with dilute nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) and then add silver nitrate ($\text{AgNO}_3$) solution.
  * **Observation for Benzyl chloride ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}$):** 
    It has a highly reactive aliphatic chlorine which readily undergoes nucleophilic hydrolysis to form benzyl alcohol and chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$). On adding $\text{AgNO}_3$, a **curdy white precipitate of silver chloride ($\text{AgCl}$)** is formed, which is soluble in ammonium hydroxide ($\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$).
    $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{Cl} + \text{NaOH} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + \text{NaCl}$$
    $$\text{NaCl} + \text{AgNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{AgCl} \downarrow \text{ (white ppt)} + \text{NaNO}_3$$
  * **Observation for Chlorobenzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl}$):** 
    Because of resonance, the chlorine is directly bonded to an $sp^2$ carbon of the benzene ring and has partial double bond character. It is unreactive and does not undergo hydrolysis under these mild conditions. Hence, **no white precipitate** is formed.

  **(b) Distinguishing Chloroform and Carbon tetrachloride:**
  * **Test:** Carbylamine Test (Isocyanide Test). Warm each compound with primary amine (such as aniline, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2$) and alcoholic potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$).
  * **Observation for Chloroform ($\text{CHCl}_3$):** 
    It reacts to form phenyl isocyanide ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NC}$), which has an **extremely offensive, foul, or highly characteristic smell**.
    $$\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + 3\text{KOH(alc)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NC} \uparrow \text{ (foul smell)} + 3\text{KCl} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  * **Observation for Carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$):** 
    It does not contain an acidic hydrogen atom and does not undergo the Carbylamine reaction. **No foul smell** is produced.

### Q21. What happens when:
**(a) n-butyl chloride is treated with alcoholic KOH?**  
**(b) Methyl chloride is treated with KCN?**  
### OR  
### Explain why the dipole moment of chlorobenzene is lower than that of cyclohexyl chloride.
* **Solution (Main Questions):**
  * **(a) n-butyl chloride with alcoholic KOH:**
    When n-butyl chloride is heated with alcoholic $\text{KOH}$, it undergoes a $\beta$-elimination (dehydrohalogenation) reaction. A hydrogen atom from the $\beta$-carbon and the chlorine atom from the $\alpha$-carbon are eliminated to yield **1-butene** as the major product.
    $$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Cl} + \text{KOH (alc.)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}=\text{CH}_2 \text{ (1-Butene)} + \text{KCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  * **(b) Methyl chloride with KCN:**
    When methyl chloride is treated with potassium cyanide ($\text{KCN}$) in aqueous ethanol, a nucleophilic substitution reaction ($\text{S}_N2$) occurs where the nucleophile cyanide ($\text{CN}^-$) displaces chloride. The major product is **methyl cyanide** (acetonitrile or ethanenitrile).
    $$\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{KCN} \xrightarrow{\text{aq. ethanol}} \text{CH}_3\text{CN} \text{ (Methyl cyanide)} + \text{KCl}$$

* **Solution (OR Option):**
  The dipole moment ($\mu = q \times d$) of chlorobenzene ($\sim 1.69\text{ D}$) is significantly lower than that of cyclohexyl chloride ($\sim 2.0\text{ D}$) due to two key reasons:
  1. **Difference in Carbon Hybridization:** 
     In chlorobenzene, the chlorine atom is attached to an $sp^2$ hybridized carbon of the benzene ring. In cyclohexyl chloride, it is attached to an $sp^3$ hybridized carbon. An $sp^2$ hybridized carbon is more electronegative than an $sp^3$ carbon due to greater $s$-character (33% vs 25%). Consequently, the $sp^2$ carbon of chlorobenzene has a greater electron-withdrawing tendency, which opposes the inductive effect of the highly electronegative chlorine atom, reducing the net polar charge separation ($q$) in the $\text{C-Cl}$ bond.
  2. **Resonance Effect (+R):** 
     In chlorobenzene, the lone pairs of electrons on the chlorine atom are in resonance conjugation with the $\pi$-electron system of the benzene ring. This imparts a partial double bond character to the $\text{C-Cl}$ bond. A double bond is shorter than a single bond. The shorter bond length ($d$) in chlorobenzene further reduces the dipole moment since $\mu$ is directly proportional to bond distance. In cyclohexyl chloride, there is no resonance, and the $\text{C-Cl}$ bond remains a longer, highly polar single bond.

### Q22. A solution prepared by dissolving $1.25\text{ g}$ of an unknown non-volatile solute in $99.0\text{ g}$ of acetone has a boiling point of $56.38\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The boiling point of pure acetone is $56.05\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the molar mass of the solute. ($K_b$ for acetone = $1.72\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$).
* **Solution:**
  1. **Calculate the elevation in boiling point ($\Delta T_b$):**
     $$\Delta T_b = T_b - T_b^\circ$$
     $$\Delta T_b = 56.38\ ^\circ\text{C} - 56.05\ ^\circ\text{C} = 0.33\ ^\circ\text{C} \text{ (or } 0.33\text{ K)}$$

  2. **Identify the given variables:**
     * Mass of solute ($w_2$) = $1.25\text{ g}$
     * Mass of solvent ($w_1$) = $99.0\text{ g}$
     * $K_b$ of acetone = $1.72\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$

  3. **Use the elevation of boiling point formula:**
     $$\Delta T_b = K_b \cdot m = K_b \cdot \frac{w_2 \cdot 1000}{M_2 \cdot w_1}$$

### Q23. Explain the following terms with suitable examples:
**(a) Abnormal molar mass**  
**(b) Van't Hoff factor ($i$)**  
**(c) Azeotropes**
* **Solution:**
  **(a) Abnormal molar mass:**
  * When the molar mass of a solute determined experimentally from its colligative properties is found to be either higher or lower than its normal (theoretical) molecular mass, it is called abnormal molar mass. 
  * This deviation occurs because of either **dissociation** or **association** of solute molecules in the solution, which changes the total number of active particles.
  * *Example:* When sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) dissolves in water, it dissociates into $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions. The double amount of particles causes the experimental colligative properties to double, which mathematically results in a calculated molecular mass of $\approx 29.25\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, exactly half of its true molar mass ($58.5\text{ g mol}^{-1}$). Conversely, acetic acid undergoes dimerization in benzene, leading to a calculated molar mass that is twice the normal value.

  **(b) Van't Hoff factor ($i$):**
  * The Van't Hoff factor ($i$) is a dimensionless coefficient introduced to account for the extent of association or dissociation of solute particles in a solution. It is defined as:
    $$i = \frac{\text{Experimental (observed) value of colligative property}}{\text{Calculated (theoretical) value assuming no association/dissociation}}$$
    $$i = \frac{\text{Actual moles of particles in solution after association/dissociation}}{\text{Moles of formula units dissolved initially}}$$
    $$i = \frac{\text{Normal (theoretical) molar mass}}{\text{Abnormal (observed) molar mass}}$$
  * *Example:* For completely dissociated sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$), $i = 2$. For a solute that undergoes complete dimerization (such as benzoic acid in benzene), $i = 0.5$.

  **(c) Azeotropes:**
  * Azeotropes are constant-boiling binary liquid mixtures that possess the exact same composition in both the liquid phase and the vapor phase. Because they boil at a single, constant temperature, they behave like pure liquids and cannot be separated into their constituent components by fractional distillation.
  * There are two types of azeotropes:
    1. **Minimum boiling azeotropes:** Formed by solutions showing large positive deviations from Raoult's law. *Example:* A mixture of 95% ethanol and 5% water by volume.
    2. **Maximum boiling azeotropes:** Formed by solutions showing large negative deviations from Raoult's law. *Example:* A mixture of 68% nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) and 32% water by mass.

### Q24. The resistance of a conductivity cell filled with $0.1\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution is $100\ \Omega$. If the resistance of the same cell filled with $0.02\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution is $520\ \Omega$, calculate the conductivity and molar conductivity of $0.02\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution. (Conductivity of $0.1\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution is $1.29\text{ S/m}$).
* **Solution:**
  1. **Find the Cell Constant ($G^*$):**
     Let $R_1$ and $\kappa_1$ be the resistance and conductivity of the $0.1\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution.
     $$R_1 = 100\ \Omega, \quad \kappa_1 = 1.29\text{ S m}^{-1}$$
     Using the relation between conductivity and resistance:
     $$\kappa = \frac{G^*}{R} \Rightarrow G^* = \kappa_1 \cdot R_1$$
     $$G^* = 1.29\text{ S m}^{-1} \times 100\ \Omega = 129\text{ m}^{-1}$$
     *(In common units, $G^* = 1.29\text{ cm}^{-1}$)*.

  2. **Calculate the Conductivity ($\kappa_2$) of the $0.02\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution:**
     Let $R_2$ be the resistance of this solution ($R_2 = 520\ \Omega$).
     $$\kappa_2 = \frac{G^*}{R_2}$$
     $$\kappa_2 = \frac{129\text{ m}^{-1}}{520\ \Omega} \approx 0.248\text{ S m}^{-1}$$
     *(In common units, $\kappa_2 = \frac{1.29\text{ cm}^{-1}}{520\ \Omega} \approx 2.48 \times 10^{-3}\text{ S cm}^{-1}$)*.

  3. **Calculate the Molar Conductivity ($\Lambda_m$) of the $0.02\text{ mol L}^{-1}$ KCl solution:**
     Concentration ($C$) = $0.02\text{ mol L}^{-1}$
     To compute in **SI units ($\text{S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$)**:
     Convert concentration from $\text{mol L}^{-1}$ to $\text{mol m}^{-3}$:
     $$C = 0.02\text{ mol L}^{-1} = 0.02 \times 1000\text{ mol m}^{-3} = 20\text{ mol m}^{-3}$$
     $$\Lambda_m = \frac{\kappa_2}{C} = \frac{0.248\text{ S m}^{-1}}{20\text{ mol m}^{-3}} = 0.0124\text{ S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1} \text{ (or } 1.24 \times 10^{-2}\text{ S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}\text{)}$$
     
     Alternatively, to compute in **common units ($\text{S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$)**:
     $$\Lambda_m = \frac{1000 \cdot \kappa_2 (\text{in S cm}^{-1})}{C (\text{in mol L}^{-1})}$$
     $$\Lambda_m = \frac{1000 \times 2.48 \times 10^{-3}}{0.02} = \frac{2.48}{0.02} = 124\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$$

  * **Answer:** 
    * Conductivity ($\kappa_2$) = **$0.248\text{ S m}^{-1}$** (or **$2.48 \times 10^{-3}\text{ S cm}^{-1}$**)
    * Molar Conductivity ($\Lambda_m$) = **$1.24 \times 10^{-2}\text{ S m}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$** (or **$124\text{ S cm}^2\text{ mol}^{-1}$**)

### Q25. State Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions. Write two applications of Kohlrausch's law in electrochemistry.
* **Solution:**
  1. **Kohlrausch's Law Statement:**
     Kohlrausch's law states that at infinite dilution (where dissociation of electrolytes is 100% complete and ion-ion interactions are negligible), the limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte ($\Lambda^\circ_m$) is equal to the sum of the individual ionic conductivities of its constituent cations and anions, each multiplied by the number of ions present in one formula unit of the electrolyte.
     $$\Lambda^\circ_m = \nu_+ \lambda^\circ_+ + \nu_- \lambda^\circ_-$$
     where:
     * $\lambda^\circ_+$ and $\lambda^\circ_-$ are the limiting molar conductivities of the cation and anion respectively.
     * $\nu_+$ and $\nu_-$ are the number of moles of cations and anions produced per mole of electrolyte.
     
     *Example:* For $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$:
     $$\Lambda^\circ_m(\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3) = 2\lambda^\circ(\text{Al}^{3+}) + 3\lambda^\circ(\text{SO}_4^{2-})$$

  2. **Two Applications of Kohlrausch's Law:**
     * **Application 1: Calculation of limiting molar conductivity ($\Lambda^\circ_m$) for weak electrolytes:**
       Since weak electrolytes (such as acetic acid, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) do not dissociate completely even at low concentrations, we cannot find $\Lambda^\circ_m$ by direct extrapolation. Kohlrausch's law allows its mathematical calculation using known $\Lambda^\circ_m$ values of strong electrolytes:
       $$\Lambda^\circ_m(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = \Lambda^\circ_m(\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}) + \Lambda^\circ_m(\text{HCl}) - \Lambda^\circ_m(\text{NaCl})$$
     * **Application 2: Calculation of degree of dissociation ($\alpha$) and dissociation constant ($K_a$) of a weak electrolyte:**
       Using the molar conductivity ($\Lambda_m$) at a concentration $C$ and limiting molar conductivity ($\Lambda^\circ_m$), we can determine:
       * Degree of dissociation: $\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda^\circ_m}$
       * Dissociation constant: $K_a = \frac{C \alpha^2}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{C \Lambda_m^2}{\Lambda^\circ_m (\Lambda^\circ_m - \Lambda_m)}$

### Q26. The rate constants of a reaction are $2.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ s}^{-1}$ at $300\text{ K}$ and $8.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ s}^{-1}$ at $320\text{ K}$. Calculate the activation energy ($E_a$) of the reaction. [Given: $R = 8.314\text{ J K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1}$, $\log_{10} 4 = 0.6021$]

  * $\log_{10}(4) = 0.6021$

  $$0.6021 = \frac{E_a}{19.147 \times 4800}$$
  $$0.6021 = \frac{E_a}{91905.6}$$
  $$E_a = 0.6021 \times 91905.6 \approx 55336.36\text{ J mol}^{-1}$$

### Q29. Case Study 1: Chemical Kinetics & Reaction Rates
* **Questions:**
  **(a) Define pseudo-first-order reaction with a suitable example.** [1]  
  **(b) For a zero-order reaction, write the rate equation and its unit for rate constant ($k$).** [1]  
  **(c) A reaction is second order with respect to a reactant A. How is the rate of reaction affected if the concentration of A is:** [2]
  **(i) doubled?**  
  **(ii) reduced to half?**

* **Solution:**
  **(a) Pseudo-first-order reaction:**
  * A chemical reaction that is of a higher molecularity (e.g. bimolecular) but behaves kinetically as a first-order reaction under specific experimental conditions (typically when one of the reactants is present in a large, overwhelming excess) is called a pseudo-first-order reaction.
  * *Example:* **Acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of ethyl acetate:**
    $$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$
    Because water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is present in a massive excess, its concentration remains virtually constant during the course of the reaction ($\approx 55.5\text{ M}$). Thus, the rate depends only on the concentration of ethyl acetate:
    $$\text{Rate} = k' [\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5][\text{H}_2\text{O}] \Rightarrow \text{Rate} = k[\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5] \quad (\text{where } k = k'[\text{H}_2\text{O}])$$

  **(b) Zero-order reaction:**
  * **Rate equation (Differential form):**
    $$\text{Rate} = -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]^0 = k$$
    *(Integrated form: $[A]_t = -kt + [A]_0$ or $k = \frac{[A]_0 - [A]_t}{t}$)*
  * **Unit of Rate Constant ($k$):**
    The unit of $k$ for a zero-order reaction is **$\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}$** (or $\text{mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$).

  **(c) Second order with respect to reactant A:**
  The rate law is:
  $$\text{Rate } (R) = k[A]^2$$
  * **(i) If concentration of A is doubled ($[A'] = 2[A]$):**
    $$R' = k[A']^2 = k(2[A])^2 = 4k[A]^2 = 4R$$
    *Answer:* The rate of reaction increases by **4 times** (quadruples).
  * **(ii) If concentration of A is reduced to half ($[A''] = 0.5[A]$):**
    $$R'' = k[A'']^2 = k\left(\frac{1}{2}[A]\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}k[A]^2 = \frac{1}{4}R$$
    *Answer:* The rate of reaction is reduced to **1/4th** (decreases by a factor of 4).

### Q30. Case Study 2: Organometallic & Polyhalogen Compounds

  * **Reasons why osmotic pressure is preferred for macromolecules:**
    1. **Easily Measurable Magnitude at Room Temperature:** Macromolecules have very high molecular masses, resulting in solutions with extremely low molar concentrations. Other colligative properties (like boiling point elevation or freezing point depression) are so small for these dilute solutions that they cannot be measured with sufficient precision. In contrast, osmotic pressure has a significantly larger and easily measurable value (often several millimeters or centimeters of liquid height) even for very dilute solutions.
    2. **Carried Out at Room Temperature:** Many macromolecules, particularly biomolecules like proteins, are thermally unstable and denature or decompose at elevated temperatures. Boiling point elevation measurements are therefore impossible. Osmotic pressure measurements can be safely carried out at standard room temperature ($25\ ^\circ\text{C}$).
    3. **Use of Molarity Instead of Molality:** Osmotic pressure is expressed in terms of molarity ($M$, moles of solute per liter of solution) rather than molality ($m$, moles of solute per kg of solvent). Molarity is far easier to prepare and measure in a laboratory setting for polymers which often have limited solubility.

  **(a) Ideal and Non-Ideal Solutions:**
  * **Ideal Solution:** A solution that obeys Raoult's law strictly over the entire range of concentration and at all temperatures. For an ideal solution:

### Q33. (a) Derive the integrated rate expression for a first-order reaction: $k = \frac{2.303}{t} \log_{10} \left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}\right)$.
### (b) Show that for a first-order reaction, time required for 99.9% completion is 10 times the half-life period ($t_{1/2}$) of the reaction.
### OR
### (a) What is collision theory of chemical reactions? Mention two main reasons why all molecular collisions do not lead to product formation.
### (b) The rate of a reaction quadruples when temperature changes from $293\text{ K}$ to $313\text{ K}$. Calculate the activation energy ($E_a$) of the reaction assuming that it does not change with temperature.

* **Solution (Main Questions):**
  **(a) Derivation of First-Order Integrated Rate Equation:**
  Consider a general first-order reaction:
  $$\text{A} \rightarrow \text{Products}$$
  According to the rate law, the rate of reaction is directly proportional to the concentration of reactant $A$ raised to power 1:
  $$\text{Rate} = -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]$$
  where $k$ is the first-order rate constant.
  
  Rearranging the differential equation to separate variables:
  $$\frac{d[A]}{[A]} = -k \, dt$$
  Integrating both sides of the equation:
  $$\int \frac{d[A]}{[A]} = -k \int dt$$
  $$\ln [A] = -kt + I \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$
  where $I$ is the constant of integration.
  
  At initial time $t = 0$, the concentration of reactant $[A] = [A]_0$ (initial concentration). Substituting these boundary conditions into Equation 1:
  $$\ln [A]_0 = -k(0) + I \Rightarrow I = \ln [A]_0$$
  Substituting the value of $I$ back into Equation 1:
  $$\ln [A] = -kt + \ln [A]_0$$
  Rearranging to solve for $kt$:
  $$kt = \ln [A]_0 - \ln [A]$$
  $$kt = \ln \left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}\right)$$
  Converting natural logarithm ($\ln$) to base-10 logarithm ($\log_{10}$):
  $$kt = 2.303 \log_{10} \left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}\right)$$
  $$k = \frac{2.303}{t} \log_{10} \left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}\right)$$
  This is the desired integrated rate expression.

  **(b) Proof that $t_{99.9\%} = 10 \cdot t_{1/2}$:**
  1. **Time for 99.9% completion ($t_{99.9\%}$):**
     If the reaction is 99.9% complete, the amount of reactant left is:
     $$[A] = [A]_0 - 0.999[A]_0 = 0.001[A]_0 = 10^{-3}[A]_0$$
     Using the first-order rate equation:
     $$t_{99.9\%} = \frac{2.303}{k} \log_{10} \left(\frac{[A]_0}{10^{-3}[A]_0}\right) = \frac{2.303}{k} \log_{10}(10^3)$$
     $$t_{99.9\%} = \frac{2.303}{k} \times 3 \log_{10}(10) = \frac{6.909}{k} \quad \text{--- (Equation I)}$$

  2. **Half-life period ($t_{1/2}$):**
     At half-life, 50% of the reactant is consumed, so $[A] = 0.5[A]_0$.
     $$t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303}{k} \log_{10} \left(\frac{[A]_0}{0.5[A]_0}\right) = \frac{2.303}{k} \log_{10}(2)$$
     Since $\log_{10}(2) \approx 0.3010$:
     $$t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303 \times 0.3010}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k} \quad \text{--- (Equation II)}$$

  3. **Compare the two time periods:**
     Divide Equation I by Equation II:
     $$\frac{t_{99.9\%}}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{\frac{6.909}{k}}{\frac{0.693}{k}} = \frac{6.909}{0.693} \approx 10$$
     $$t_{99.9\%} = 10 \times t_{1/2}$$
     *(Hence proved)*.

* **Solution (OR Option):**
  **(a) Collision Theory of Chemical Reactions:**
  * **Core Concept:** Developed by Max Trautz and William Lewis, it states that chemical reactions occur as a result of collisions between reacting molecules. The rate of reaction depends on the number of collisions per second per unit volume (collision frequency, $Z_{AB}$) and the fraction of collisions that are effective.
    $$\text{Rate} = Z_{AB} \cdot P \cdot e^{-E_a / RT}$$
    where $Z_{AB}$ is the collision frequency, $P$ is the probability/steric factor, and $e^{-E_a/RT}$ represents the fraction of molecules with kinetic energy equal to or greater than the activation energy.
  * **Two main reasons why all molecular collisions do not lead to product formation:**
    1. **Energy Barrier (Activation Energy):** Colliding molecules must possess a minimum kinetic energy, called the threshold energy, to break their existing bonds and form new ones. If the kinetic energy of colliding molecules is less than the activation energy, the collision is ineffective, and the molecules bounce apart.
    2. **Orientation Barrier (Proper Orientation):** The reactant molecules must collide with a specific spatial orientation so that the reactive atoms are brought together in the correct arrangement. If the orientation is improper, the collision will not lead to bond formation and product generation even if the molecules have sufficient energy.

  **(b) Activation Energy ($E_a$) Calculation:**
  * **Identify Given Values:**
    * Initial temperature ($T_1$) = $293\text{ K}$
    * Final temperature ($T_2$) = $313\text{ K}$
    * Rate of reaction quadruples, so $\frac{k_2}{k_1} = 4.0$
    * $R = 8.314\text{ J K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1}$
    * $\log_{10}(4) = 0.6021$
  * **Apply Arrhenius Equation:**
    $$\log_{10} \left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_a}{2.303 \cdot R} \left[\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 \cdot T_2}\right]$$
    $$0.6021 = \frac{E_a}{2.303 \times 8.314} \left[\frac{313 - 293}{293 \times 313}\right]$$
    $$0.6021 = \frac{E_a}{19.147} \left[\frac{20}{91709}\right]$$
    $$0.6021 \times 19.147 \times 91709 = 20 \cdot E_a$$
    $$1057252 = 20 \cdot E_a$$
    $$E_a = \frac{1057252}{20} \approx 52863\text{ J mol}^{-1} \approx 52.86\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$$
  
  * **Answer:** The activation energy ($E_a$) of the reaction is **$52.86\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$**.

### Q34. (a) Account for the following observations:
**(i) Haloarenes are extremely less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution reactions.**  
**(ii) Alkyl halides, though polar, are immiscible with water.**  
**(iii) Racemic mixture is optically inactive.**  
### (b) Primary alkyl halide $\text{C}_4\text{H}_9\text{Br}$ (A) reacted with alcoholic KOH to give compound (B). Compound (B) is reacted with HBr to give (C) which is an isomer of (A). When (A) is reacted with sodium metal it gives compound (D), $\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18}$ which is different from the compound formed when n-butyl bromide is reacted with sodium. Write the structural formula of (A), (B), (C), and (D).

* **Solution:**
  **(a) Account for the following observations:**
  * **(i) Haloarenes low reactivity towards nucleophilic substitution:**
    1. **Resonance Effect:** The lone pair of electrons on the halogen atom is in resonance with the $\pi$-electrons of the benzene ring. This imparts a partial double bond character to the $\text{C-X}$ bond, making it shorter, stronger, and much more difficult to cleave than the single $\text{C-X}$ bond in haloalkanes.
    2. **Difference in Carbon Hybridization:** In haloarenes, the halogen is attached to an $sp^2$ hybridized carbon (which has 33% $s$-character and is more electronegative), whereas in haloalkanes it is attached to an $sp^3$ hybridized carbon (25% $s$-character). The highly electronegative $sp^2$ carbon holds the C-X electron pair very tightly, shortening the bond and increasing bond strength.
    3. **Instability of Phenyl Cation:** Cleavage of the C-X bond would yield a phenyl cation. The phenyl cation cannot be resonance stabilized because the vacant $sp^2$ orbital is perpendicular to the aromatic $\pi$-system.
    4. **Steric and Electrostatic Repulsion:** The incoming electron-rich nucleophile is repelled by the electron-dense aromatic $\pi$-electron cloud of the benzene ring, hindering backside attack.
  * **(ii) Alkyl halides, though polar, are immiscible with water:**
    To dissolve in water, a solute must form strong intermolecular attractions with water molecules to overcome the solute-solute interactions and break the strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules. 
    Alkyl halides are polar molecules held together by dipole-dipole attractions. However, they cannot form hydrogen bonds with water. The new dipole-dipole interactions formed between alkyl halides and water molecules are much weaker than the strong, pre-existing hydrogen bonds in pure water. Since the energy released is insufficient to break water's hydrogen bonds, alkyl halides remain immiscible in water.
  * **(iii) Racemic mixture is optically inactive:**
    A racemic mixture contains an equimolar (50:50) ratio of two enantiomers (the dextro and levo forms). The clockwise optical rotation of plane-polarized light caused by one enantiomer is exactly equal and opposite to the counter-clockwise optical rotation caused by the other enantiomer. This net cancellation of optical rotation is called **external compensation**, resulting in zero net optical rotation and optical inactivity.

  **(b) Organic Compound Identification:**
  Let's deduce the compounds step-by-step:
  1. **Identify (A):**
     * (A) is a primary alkyl halide with the molecular formula $\text{C}_4\text{H}_9\text{Br}$.
     * The only two primary alkyl halide isomers of $\text{C}_4\text{H}_9\text{Br}$ are:
       1. n-butyl bromide: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Br}$
       2. Isobutyl bromide (1-bromo-2-methylpropane): $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH-CH}_2\text{Br}$
     * We are given that when (A) reacts with sodium metal (Wurtz reaction), it gives (D) ($\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18}$) which is **different** from the product formed when n-butyl bromide reacts with sodium. This means **(A) cannot be n-butyl bromide**.
     * Therefore, (A) must be **Isobutyl bromide (1-Bromo-2-methylpropane)**:
       $$\text{(A): } (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH-CH}_2\text{-Br} \quad \text{or} \quad \text{CH}_3\text{-CH(CH}_3)\text{-CH}_2\text{-Br}$$

  2. **Identify (B):**
     * (A) reacts with alcoholic $\text{KOH}$ via elimination to give compound (B):
       $$(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH-CH}_2\text{-Br} + \text{KOH (alc.)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}=\text{CH}_2 \text{ (2-Methylpropene)} + \text{KBr} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
     * Therefore, (B) is **2-Methylpropene** (isobutylene):
       $$\text{(B): } (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}=\text{CH}_2$$

  3. **Identify (C):**
     * (B) is reacted with $\text{HBr}$ (Markovnikov addition) to give (C):
       $$(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{HBr} \rightarrow (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br} \text{ (tert-butyl bromide)}$$
     * (C) ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_9\text{Br}$) is a tertiary alkyl halide, which is indeed an isomer of (A). This matches perfectly.
       $$\text{(C): } (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C-Br} \quad \text{or} \quad \text{CH}_3\text{-C(CH}_3)_2\text{-Br}$$

  4. **Identify (D):**
     * (A) reacts with sodium in dry ether (Wurtz reaction) to yield (D):

     * The molecular formula of 2,5-dimethylhexane is $\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18}$, which is isomeric to and different from n-octane (the product from the Wurtz reaction of n-butyl bromide).
       $$\text{(D): } (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}(\text{CH}_3)_2$$

### Q35. (a) Explain the mechanism of $\text{S}_N2$ substitution reaction of methyl bromide with aqueous NaOH solution with energy profile/transition state representation.
### (b) Write the environmental effects and uses of:
**(i) Freon-12 (Dichlorodifluoromethane)**  
**(ii) DDT (p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane)**

* **Solution:**
  **(a) Mechanism of $\text{S}_N2$ reaction of methyl bromide with aqueous NaOH:**
  1. **Core Mechanism:**
     The substitution of methyl bromide ($\text{CH}_3\text{Br}$) by aqueous $\text{NaOH}$ follows a **Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular ($\text{S}_N2$)** mechanism. This is a single-step concerted process without any chemical intermediates.
     * The nucleophile ($\text{OH}^-$ ion from aqueous $\text{NaOH}$) attacks the electrophilic carbon of methyl bromide from the **backside** (opposite to the leaving bromide group) to minimize steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion from the highly negative bromine atom.
     * As the nucleophile ($\text{OH}^-$) starts to form a bond with carbon, the carbon-halogen ($\text{C-Br}$) bond starts to break simultaneously.

  2. **Transition State:**
     This leads to the formation of a high-energy, unstable **transition state** ($\ddagger$), where the central carbon is partially bonded to both the incoming $-\text{OH}$ group and the outgoing $-\text{Br}$ group. 
     In this transition state:
     * The central carbon is in a pentacoordinated state with $sp^2$ hybridization.
     * The three hydrogen atoms lie in a single plane perpendicular to the $\text{O-C-Br}$ axis.
     * As the bromide ion ($\text{Br}^-$) fully departs, the hydrogen atoms flip to the opposite side (similar to an umbrella blowing inside out in a strong wind). This results in a product (**methanol**) with **complete inversion of configuration** (Walden Inversion).
     
     $$\text{OH}^- + \text{CH}_3\text{-Br} \rightarrow \left[ \text{HO}\cdots\text{C}(\text{H}_3)\cdots\text{Br} \right]^{\ddagger} \rightarrow \text{HO-CH}_3 + \text{Br}^-$$

  3. **Energy Profile Diagram:**
     The potential energy curve exhibits a single peak corresponding to the high-energy Transition State. There are no valleys because no intermediate is formed.
     
     ![S_N2 Energy Profile](sn2_energy_profile.png)

  **(b) Uses and Environmental Effects:**
  * **(i) Freon-12 (Dichlorodifluoromethane, $\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2$):**
    * **Uses:** 
      Widely used as an efficient refrigerant in domestic refrigerators, deep freezers, and automotive air conditioning systems. It was also used as an aerosol propellant in sprays and as a blowing agent for plastics.
    * **Environmental Effects:** 
      Freon-12 is highly stable and does not break down in the lower atmosphere. It gradually diffuses into the stratosphere, where intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation homolytically cleaves the $\text{C-Cl}$ bond, releasing chlorine free radicals ($\text{Cl}^\bullet$). These chlorine radicals catalyze the conversion of ozone ($\text{O}_3$) into oxygen ($\text{O}_2$), depleting the protective ozone layer. This ozone depletion allows harmful UV-B radiation to reach the Earth's surface, causing skin cancers, cataracts, and severely damaging marine and agricultural ecosystems.

  * **(ii) DDT (p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane):**
    * **Uses:** 
      A powerful and highly effective contact insecticide. It was extensively used globally to eliminate mosquitoes carrying malaria and lice carrying typhus, and as an agricultural pesticide to protect crops from insects.
    * **Environmental Effects:** 
      DDT is highly stable, extremely non-biodegradable, and highly fat-soluble. It does not dissolve in water but readily dissolves in lipids. Consequently, when ingested by animals, it cannot be metabolized or excreted easily and instead accumulates in adipose (fatty) tissues. This leads to **biomagnification** (concentration increases significantly at higher levels of the food chain). In birds, high DDT concentrations interfere with calcium metabolism, leading to abnormally thin eggshells that break prematurely during incubation, causing a drastic decline in bird populations. Due to its toxicity and environmental persistence, DDT is banned or heavily restricted in most countries.
